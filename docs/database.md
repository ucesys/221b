# The database

221B keeps its records in PostgreSQL (Phase 13); until 0.13 it kept them in ConfigMaps. ConfigMaps hold 1 MiB each, are read and written whole, and gave every record set its own cap; PostgreSQL holds the records as rows of the [data model](data-model.md)'s tables. This page says how the database is chosen and run, what the console does with it, and how a domain's records move.

**The database is the only store** (DB-69, the owner, November 20, 2026). Every domain has moved, the chart runs a bundled database by default, and a console does not run without one. A ConfigMap of a release before 0.13 is read once, by the copy at the first start with the database, and by nothing else, and the console deletes it once the database holds every record set it held (DB-72). The table says what each domain keeps in the database and what it keeps elsewhere, and why.

| Domain | In the database | Kept elsewhere |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Activity log | the audit log (`audit_events`) and the Toolbox's session records (`toolbox_sessions`): the whole domain | |
| Platform | the shared settings (`settings`, scopes `general`, `sherlock`, `watson`) and the acknowledged alerts (`alert_acks`); the repositories (`repositories`); the backups' settings (scope `backup`) and runs (`backup_runs`); getting started's dismissed steps, Settings › Email and the case work's settings (scopes `setup`, `email`, `cases`); the credentials' list (`credentials`, and the scope `credentials-migration`), which was a key of the credentials' Secret: every field of a credential but its secret, when the secrets are kept in a Secret; the applications (`applications`, `application_commands`), which were a key of each application's Secret: every field of an application but its values file, its parameters and its commands' parameters, when the connections are kept in Secrets; the cluster connections (`clusters`), which were a key of each connection's Secret: every field of a connection but its credential, when the connections are kept in Secrets | what stays in Secrets for good: every credential's secret, in the credentials' Secret; every application's values, parameters and commands' parameters, in the application's Secret; and every connection's credential (the token, kubeconfig, client certificate and key, OIDC secrets, with the CA certificate and the rest that is read with them), in the connection's Secret. `clusters` holds a row with only its id for each cluster another table points at whose connection it does not keep, and `credentials` one for each credential something names that the list does not have |

## Choosing a database

`database.mode` in the chart's values:

| Mode | What runs | For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `bundled` | The chart's own PostgreSQL beside the console, from the image `221b-postgres`: one member, or three with `database.bundled.ha`. **The default.** | An installation with no PostgreSQL to use. |
| `external` | Nothing of the chart's; the console connects to a PostgreSQL you run. | A managed service (RDS, Cloud SQL, Azure), your own Patroni or CloudNativePG cluster, a central database platform. |

There is no mode without a database: `none`, which kept the records in ConfigMaps until 0.13, was removed (DB-69) and is refused with what to set instead. The chart refuses what cannot work: a mode that is neither of the two; values kept from a release before 0.13 by `helm upgrade --reuse-values`, which have no `database` block (upgrade with `--reset-then-reuse-values` or your values file); `external` with no host, or with `sslMode: disable` and no `allowInsecure`; one bundled member in a namespace labelled `221b.io/environment=production` without `database.bundled.acknowledgeNoHA`; and a release whose mode changes between `bundled` and `external`, which is a migration (below), not a value.

## The bundled database

One release, two images: `221b` stays distroless and holds no database; `221b-postgres` holds PostgreSQL 18 and [Patroni](https://patroni.readthedocs.io/) from [Wolfi](https://wolfi.dev)'s packages and [WAL-G](https://github.com/wal-g/wal-g)'s own release, and runs as user 999 with a read-only root filesystem ([The image](#the-image) below). Both are built for linux/amd64 and linux/arm64, scanned (any high or critical finding fails the build), signed and attested, and published under the same tags; the chart runs `221b-postgres` at its appVersion ([releasing.md](releasing.md)).

- **Members.** A StatefulSet of one pod, or three with `ha: true`, each with its own volume (`storage.size`, 10 GiB by default). Three members are kept on different nodes, spread over zones where there are zones, with a disruption budget of one. Turning `ha` on later adds the two standbys while the primary keeps serving.
- **Who leads.** Patroni keeps its leader lock and the cluster's state in ConfigMaps of the console's namespace and labels each pod with its role; the Service `<release>-db` selects the pod labelled `primary`, and is the console's one address. A dead primary is replaced within about 30 seconds; a planned switch takes a few. While the Kubernetes API is away the primary keeps serving as long as it reaches every member (failsafe mode).
- **Commits.** With `synchronous: quorum` (the default) a commit waits for one of the two standbys, so a failover loses nothing committed, and losing one standby stops nothing. One member alone waits for nobody.
- **Access.** The chart makes three passwords once, in the Secret `<release>-db`, and keeps them through upgrades and an uninstall, as the volumes are kept: the superuser's and the replication role's are Patroni's, and the console is given only its own role's, `app_221b`, which owns the database `221b` and nothing else. Connections are TLS with a certificate the chart makes once (`<release>-db-tls`; or yours, `tls.secretName`), verified by the console (`verify-full`), and passwords are SCRAM. A network policy admits the console and the members to PostgreSQL, and the members alone to Patroni's API.
- **Kubernetes rights.** A Role in the namespace: ConfigMaps, and its own pods to label. Nothing cluster-wide, no CRDs, no operator.
- **Backups.** PostgreSQL archives every completed segment of its write-ahead log through WAL-G, and a `backup` container in each pod takes a base backup on one member on a schedule: `backup.schedule`, a cron expression of five fields in UTC (`"0 2 * * *"` every day at 02:00, `"0 */6 * * *"` every six hours, `@weekly`), or, when it is empty, every day at `backup.dailyAt` (`"02:00"`, the value of before, read as `"0 2 * * *"`). The container runs `221b-backup`, a small Go program built into the image (DB-84), which replaced the shell script of before and does what it did: which member takes it, a member that becomes the primary of a volume taking one at once, a time missed while Patroni did not answer or after a failure taken later the same day, and a scheduled time of an earlier day (UTC) that no member was running at not made up at a start. `backup.retainBaseBackups` (7) are kept, with the log between them, so the database can be restored to any moment since the oldest. Both are encrypted before they leave the pod, with the key `backup-key` of the Secret `<release>-db`.
  - **To a bucket** (`backup.s3`): every member writes to the one archive. The standby whose name sorts first takes the base backup, or the primary when it is alone.
  - **To a volume** (no bucket): each member has a backup volume of its own, and the log goes to the primary's. So the primary takes the base backup, and a member that becomes the primary after another one was takes one at once: its volume has no log for the time in between. The first primary takes one as soon as it runs. After a failover the time before it can be restored only from the old primary's volume; with three members, a bucket is the better place. A volume does not survive losing the cluster's storage, and the chart's notes say so.
- **Restore.** A new installation starts from the archive with `database.bundled.restore.fromBackup=true`, to the newest point or to `restore.targetTime` (RFC 3339, in quotes, with a time zone: `"2026-11-20T02:19:29Z"` or with an offset; the chart refuses a time without one and hands PostgreSQL the offset in numbers, since PostgreSQL refuses `Z` there); remove the value after the first install. **For a restore in place**, after `helm uninstall` delete Patroni's ConfigMaps too (`<release>-db-config`, `-leader`, `-failover`, `-sync`, `-failsafe`): they are Patroni's, not the chart's, and outlive the uninstall, and a new installation that finds them takes itself for the old cluster. A restore needs what the backups were made with:
  - **The key.** The archive is read with the `backup-key` of the old installation's Secret `<release>-db`. Under the same release name and namespace the kept Secret is found again and nothing is needed. Under another name, in another namespace or in another cluster, the chart would make a new key, and the restore would fail: copy the old Secret there first and name it in `database.bundled.existingSecret`. Copy it whole, since the restored database knows the old passwords.
  - **The archive.** A bucket is reached from anywhere with the same `backup.s3` values. A backup volume belongs to its release: it is found again only under the same release name and namespace, where the claim `backup-<release>-db-0` is kept. The restore reads the volume of `<release>-db-0`, so that member must be the primary before the uninstall, with a base backup of its own: after an upgrade that restarted the database's pods another member usually leads (`patronictl list`), and `patronictl switchover --leader <it> --candidate <release>-db-0 --force` puts it back; `db-0` takes a base backup at once. To restore there, uninstall, delete the data claim `data-<release>-db-0` (and those of the other members), keep the backup claim and the Secret, and install with `restore.fromBackup=true`.
  - Keep a copy of the Secret outside the cluster: without `backup-key` the backups cannot be read by anyone.
  - **After a restore that failed.** A restore that PostgreSQL cannot start from (a wrong `targetTime`, a key that does not open the archive) leaves the database's pod starting over and over with `Failed to bootstrap cluster` in its log, and `helm install --wait` gives up. Nothing of the archive is changed by it. Correct the value and run `helm upgrade` with the same restore values: the member starts again from the archive, not from what the failed attempt left. Each failed attempt moves the data directory it had begun aside, to `pgdata.failed` beside `pgdata` on the member's data volume (`/var/lib/postgresql/data/pgdata.failed` in the pod), in place of the one before, so there is one of them, the size of the base backup it had fetched (40 MB in the seventh cluster test, beside a 249 MB `pgdata`). Nothing reads it afterwards, and nothing removes it: it stays through the restore that works, restarts of the pod and upgrades, and goes only with the data claim. Once the restored database serves, remove it by hand: `kubectl exec <release>-db-0 -c postgres -- rm -rf /var/lib/postgresql/data/pgdata.failed`. Look at it first only if the restore still fails and the log does not say why: its `postgresql.conf` and `log` are what PostgreSQL was given.

## The image

`deploy/postgres` builds `221b-postgres`: PostgreSQL and Patroni as Wolfi packages them, assembled with [apko](https://github.com/chainguard-dev/apko) straight from `packages.wolfi.dev`, with no registry image of anyone else's underneath; WAL-G's release; 221B's scripts and backup program. It became the image in 0.13.0 (the owner, November 21, 2026; ADR-169); the Debian image the development builds of 0.13 ran before is gone, and a database it made runs on this one unchanged ([upgrading.md](upgrading.md)). Its labels say it is built from Wolfi packages and name what 221B added; it is not a Chainguard image and carries no Chainguard name or mark.

- **What is inside.** PostgreSQL 18 (`postgresql-18`, `postgresql-18-client`), Patroni with its Kubernetes and psycopg support (`patroni`), Python 3.13 (`restore.sh` uses it), `bash`, `busybox`, certificates and time zones, glibc with its `C.UTF-8` locale; WAL-G in `/usr/local/bin/wal-g`; `entrypoint.sh`, `post-bootstrap.sh`, `restore.sh` and `221b-backup` in `/usr/local/bin`. PostgreSQL's programs are in `/usr/libexec/postgresql18`, linked from `/usr/bin`. There are no `contrib` modules (the console uses none; `amcheck` among them) and no `locale` program (initdb warns once, and uses glibc's own `C.UTF-8`). Its socket would go to `/tmp`, so the entrypoint names `/var/run/postgresql`. Wolfi's Patroni brings boto3 and botocore (123 MB) for its AWS features, and its PostgreSQL links LLVM for the JIT (146 MB): the image is about 535 MB (175 MB compressed) for amd64, 528 MB for arm64, which the owner accepted.
- **How it is built.** `apko.yaml` names the packages by major only and the user 999. `make postgres-lock` takes Wolfi's current packages, writes their exact versions for amd64 and arm64 to `apko.lock.json` (committed beside it), and writes the digest of the base apko builds from that lock into the Dockerfile's `WOLFI_BASE_DIGEST`: the same lock gives the same digest, so every build checks the pin by building it again. `make image-postgres` (`PLATFORM=linux/amd64` or `linux/arm64`) builds the base from the lock, pushes it to a registry on 127.0.0.1 (crane's, started when nothing answers there; `WOLFI_BASE_REPO` names another), and builds the Dockerfile from it by digest; `hack/wolfi-postgres.sh publish` builds both platforms as one index and pushes it, as CI and the release do. The Dockerfile's last stage has no `RUN` step, so arm64 builds without emulation. `hack/wolfi-postgres.sh` does each step and says what it reads; its tools (apko, melange, crane, oras, cosign) are pinned there to releases at least two weeks old, each checked by SHA-256. A plain `docker build deploy/postgres` does not work: the base and WAL-G come from the script.
- **WAL-G is not Wolfi's.** Wolfi's `wal-g-pg` is built without libsodium, and the chart encrypts every backup with it (`WALG_LIBSODIUM_KEY`): it refuses to archive anything. The image takes WAL-G's own release (v3.0.10), its files checked by SHA-256 in the Dockerfile. Once Wolfi's build has libsodium, `wal-g-pg` goes into `apko.yaml` and that stage goes.
- **Moving to newer packages** is `make postgres-lock`, a commit of the lock and the Dockerfile, and the same tests as any change; nothing moves by itself between two builds of one commit. The scan in CI fails on any high or critical finding, with a fix or without: an unfixed one in Wolfi's packages is fixed by moving the lock once Wolfi has the fix.
- **The copy of the packages each release keeps.** Wolfi removes a version six months after a newer one ships, so a later rebuild of a release could not find its packages there. Each release's job (`release.yml`) keeps them: it downloads the locked `.apk` files of both architectures, checks each against the lock (the hashes of its control and data parts), makes an RSA key pair for that release alone (`hack/wolfi-postgres.sh keygen`), signs an `APKINDEX` for each architecture with it, and deletes the private key: it is never stored, uploaded or reused, and nothing can be signed with it afterwards. The public key goes into the copy (`keys/221b-packages-<release>.rsa.pub`, the name the index gives it), with the lock and WAL-G's release files; the lot is pushed as one OCI artifact with `oras` to `ghcr.io/ucesys/221b-postgres-packages:<release>` and signed with cosign, keylessly, the release workflow's identity in the certificate and the signature in Sigstore's transparency log. That signature is what vouches for the key inside.

### Building an old release again

From the release's tag, with Docker, network access to `ghcr.io` (not to `packages.wolfi.dev`), and nothing of the owner's:

```sh
git checkout v0.13.0
make postgres-rebuild WOLFI_PACKAGES=0.13.0 TAG=0.13.0-rebuilt     # the copy's tag, or its digest sha256:...
```

`hack/wolfi-postgres.sh rebuild` resolves the tag to a digest and works by digest from then on. It checks the copy's signature: a certificate of `https://github.com/ucesys/221b/.github/workflows/release.yml@refs/tags/v<version>` issued to GitHub Actions (`https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com`), or it stops. It pulls the artifact by that digest, refuses a copy of another lock than the tree's, and runs apko against the copy with the one public key the copy carries as its only key (Wolfi's is not given, so an index signed by anyone else is refused), every package pinned to its locked version. It checks that each architecture's installed packages are exactly the lock's (name, version, checksum), then builds the image from that base with WAL-G from the copy. A rebuilt base has another digest than the release's, with the same packages: its keyring, its repository file and the order of its package database differ. For tests, `COSIGN_PUB` checks the signature with a cosign key instead of the workflow's identity, and `WOLFI_PACKAGES_PUBKEY` names the key to trust instead of the copy's.

- **Tested** on November 21, 2026: the image ran three members in a kind cluster through a switchover, a failover, base backups and a restore to a point in time with the chart's defaults; a database made by the Debian image of the development builds was switched to it in place (three members, rolling, nothing lost; [upgrading.md](upgrading.md)) and a Debian-made base backup was restored by it to a point in time; and a rebuild from a copy of the packages alone, with `packages.wolfi.dev` unreachable, gave a base whose packages were exactly the lock's. Scanned with grype 0.122.1: no critical or high finding on either platform, 12 medium or lower. Before the switch the Debian image had 22 critical and 200 high findings without a fix.

## An external database

The console needs, of a PostgreSQL you run:

- **PostgreSQL 16 or later.** The console checks at start and does not start on an older one.
- **A database and a role that owns it**, made beforehand (`database.external.database`, `user`; `221b` and `app_221b` by default). The console makes and changes its own tables as that role; it needs no superuser, no replication right and nothing outside its database. No extension is needed.
- **One address that reaches the primary** (`host`, `port`): for CloudNativePG the cluster's `-rw` Service, for Patroni the primary's Service or a load balancer that follows it. The console connects again when the primary changes.
- **TLS.** `sslMode` is `verify-full` by default, with the CA in `caSecret` (key `ca.crt`) unless a public CA signed the server's certificate. `clientCertSecret` (`tls.crt`, `tls.key`) is for a server that asks for a client certificate.
- **The password** in a Secret (`passwordSecret.name` and `key`). It is read at every new connection, so a rotated Secret needs no restart. Or no password: a token of the cloud's IAM (below).
- **Connections.** `maxConnections` (20) is what all the console's replicas open between them; each replica also holds up to four direct sessions for its listener and its locks.
- **`track_commit_timestamp = on`** is recommended; the console warns when it is off.

**A token of the cloud's IAM in place of a password** (`iamAuth`, DB-83). On RDS, Aurora PostgreSQL and Cloud SQL the console can sign in with a token made from its own identity, so no password is kept in the cluster. The token is made for each new connection, in the pool's `BeforeConnect` where the password file is read otherwise; it is never logged, and is sent over TLS only. `iamAuth` with `passwordSecret.name`, or with `sslMode: disable`, is refused by the chart and by the console.

- **`iamAuth: aws`** (RDS, Aurora). An RDS IAM authentication token: a request to connect as `user`, signed with AWS Signature Version 4 for the service `rds-db` in `aws.region` and valid for 15 minutes, as the AWS SDKs' `BuildAuthToken` makes it. Signing it calls nothing; the credentials are the ServiceAccount's own (IRSA: the annotation `eks.amazonaws.com/role-arn` under `serviceAccount.annotations`; or EKS Pod Identity, which needs no annotation), kept until five minutes before they expire, as the backups to S3 and Bedrock keep theirs. The operator makes: the database's IAM authentication turned on; the role, with `rds-db:connect` on `arn:aws:rds-db:<region>:<account>:dbuser:<DbiResourceId>/<user>`; and in the database, the user with `GRANT rds_iam TO <user>`, owning the console's database. `sslMode: verify-full` with the RDS CA bundle in `caSecret`. RDS limits new connections signed in this way to about 200 a second, far above what the console opens.
- **`iamAuth: gcp`** (Cloud SQL for PostgreSQL). An OAuth2 access token of the ServiceAccount's Google identity, with the scope `sqlservice.login` (the one Google's Cloud SQL connectors ask for), from the default credentials: GKE Workload Identity (the annotation `iam.gke.io/gcp-service-account: <gsa>@<project>.iam.gserviceaccount.com` under `serviceAccount.annotations`), or a key file named by `GOOGLE_APPLICATION_CREDENTIALS`. A token lasts an hour; the console keeps it until a minute before it expires and asks for a new one then. The operator makes: the instance's flag `cloudsql.iam_authentication` on; the Google service account with `roles/cloudsql.instanceUser`; its database user (`gcloud sql users create <gsa>@<project>.iam --type=cloud_iam_service_account`), owning the console's database; `user` is that name, `<gsa>@<project>.iam`. The console connects to the instance's address directly, not through the Cloud SQL Auth Proxy or connector. Cloud SQL's server certificate names the instance and not its address, so give `sslMode: verify-ca` with the instance's server CA in `caSecret`.
- **With the network policy on**, the console reaches AWS STS on 443 for IRSA (open with `sherlock.egress`, or list it under `networkPolicy.outboundCIDRs`); on GKE the metadata server, `169.254.169.254/32` on port 80, and with GKE Dataplane V2 also `169.254.169.252/32` on port 988, under `networkPolicy.outboundCIDRs` and `outboundPorts`.

**Poolers.** The console listens for notices (`LISTEN`) and takes session advisory locks; PgBouncer in transaction mode keeps neither. Session mode, or no pooler, needs nothing. With a pooler in transaction mode, give `listenerHost`, a direct address: the listener, the locks and the migrations use it, and everything else goes through the pooler. The console's own pool is small; most installations need no pooler.

Availability and backups of an external database are yours. The console's own backup (Administration › Backup) works in every mode and is what moves an installation between modes.

## What the console does with it

- **At start** it needs a database: one given no `-db-host` stops at once and says what to set (DB-02, DB-69). It connects (trying for two minutes, since a bundled database may still be starting), checks the version, and brings the schema to its own: the steps are embedded in the binary and applied in order behind one advisory lock, so replicas that start together migrate one after another. Within a release line the steps only add, so the replicas of the release before go on working during a rollout, and going back one release is safe. The console does not listen until this is done, and the chart's startup probe allows for it (seven and a half minutes), so the liveness probe does not stop a console that is waiting for its database. **When the database has not answered in the two minutes, the console exits** saying why, the pod starts again, and nothing listens for as long as the database stays away: a console does not start without its database, and no value lets it (the option a build of this phase had, DB-67 and ADR-166, was withdrawn by the owner on November 20, 2026).
- **No replica leads the database's work.** A scheduled time is claimed by inserting its row, and the replica whose insert goes in runs it; a pass over everything runs behind an advisory lock of its name; a change the other replicas should see is announced with `NOTIFY` and looked for anyway every `database.pollInterval` (30 seconds), so a lost notice delays a change and never loses it. The Lease that elects the replica reading the clusters stays until the conversations and cards it protects have moved too.
- **While the database is away** the console stays ready and goes on serving what does not need it: sign-in, and every page read live from Kubernetes. **Sign-in** needs only the accounts' Secret, the directory or the provider, and somewhere to keep the session. That place is the database (the table `auth_sessions`, DB-66); with `connections.store: memory`, the replica's memory. (A release before 0.13 kept the sessions in Secrets, and its store was the Kubernetes API server.) When it cannot be written, the session is kept in its cookie instead, signed with the key in the Secret `<release>-session-key`, and every replica takes it without the store (DB-65). The sign-in page then says so: the session lasts `auth.outageSession`, 30 minutes from sign-in, and is not extended by use; at that time the person signs in again. At the first request after the store is back it becomes a stored session (a row), without a new sign-in, **which ends when the signed one would have and no later**: the exchange is in the audit log (`session.exchange`), and gives no more than the signed session had. While the store is away the session cannot be ended everywhere at once: a sign-out ends it on the replica that served it, a password reset on the replicas that hold it, and a local account disabled or deleted loses it at once everywhere. When the store answers, the ending is written there (a row of `auth_sessions` whose `method` is `ended`, kept until the signed session would have expired and then removed by the sweep; it is no session, and the Users page and `b221_sessions` do not count it), and a replica that did not see the sign-out refuses the signed cookie and makes no row of it. An ending that no replica remembers any longer (the replica that served the sign-out restarted before the database was back) is lost: that cookie is taken until its 30 minutes are over, and never after. A person already signed in goes on, on the replica that has seen them, from that replica's memory; on another replica they are asked to sign in, and get a signed session. **Nothing waits for a database that is away** (DB-14): while the database's health says it is away, every call to it returns "the database is unavailable" at once, without a connection being tried, so a page that needs it says so in milliseconds and a page that does not (the home page, a cluster's nodes and pods, `/metrics`) is not held by it. What finds the database away is a probe, a connection of the console's own given 2 seconds, asked for by the first call that fails or has waited 2 seconds, by the listener's connection breaking, or by the check made every 10 seconds; when it fails the console closes every connection it has to the database, so that what was already waiting returns too. A database that refuses connections is found away in milliseconds. One that swallows what it is sent, which is what the bundled database's Service looks like with its pods gone, **holds the first requests to meet it for up to 4 seconds, once**. The probe is then made every second, and the console is back within 1 to 3 seconds of the database answering it, without a restart. A slow query on a database that answers changes nothing for anyone else: a call's failure asks for the probe, and only the probe's failure marks the database away. A sign-out during the outage is refused at once on the replica that served it, which removes the session's row as soon as the database's health says it is back; the other replicas, which could not be told, take the session until then (a replica restarted meanwhile forgets what it was to remove, and the row then lives until its idle limit). Last activity is not written during the outage; each replica counts idle time from what it has seen. A person in so many groups that the session does not fit a cookie is told that a local account can sign in. A page that needs the database says it is unavailable, in the console's words and no others ("the database is unavailable; sign-in and the pages read live from the clusters still work"; the driver's reason, with the database's address, is in the console's log); a write fails with the same sentence and is never queued in memory, except the audit log, whose newest 2,000 events wait. `b221_database_up` is 0, and `/metrics` goes on answering in milliseconds, since it reads memory only (the count of sessions and Sherlock's spend are then what was last read); the log says when the database left, with the driver's own reason, and when it came back. **The clusters stay connected** (DB-64): every connection that is running keeps running with the credential it has, and the local cluster never needs the database at all; the log says once that the saved connections cannot be read, and once that they can again. Adding, changing or removing a connection is refused with why, and so are a test of a saved connection and a backup of the connections, which read the saved credential. What another replica changed just before is read when the database answers. A console that starts while the database is away does not start (DB-02), so the question of connections that were never loaded does not arise.
- **What the log says while the database is away** (DB-15). The database itself says when it goes (*the database is unavailable; pages that need it say so at once, and the rest go on*) and when it answers again. What reads or writes it every few seconds says its failure once, with `repeats="said again only once it works"`, and once more when it works again (*the AI configuration is read again*, *the saved settings are read again*, *the audit log is saved again, with the events queued meanwhile*); a page answered 503 for it is logged once for its route until the route answers again. A failure of another kind is logged each time.
- **What a person sees while the database is away** (DB-15). On every page, under the top bar, a banner: *The database is unavailable. Sign-in and the pages read live from the clusters still work. What the console keeps in the database cannot be read or changed until it is back: the Inbox, cases and reports, jobs and their runs, Mycroft, Automation, applications, credentials, the audit log and the settings.* It says since when and, to a SuperAdmin, why in the console's words (it refuses connections, it does not answer). It appears at the first call the console refuses, or within half a minute on a page that asks for nothing, and goes by itself within 10 seconds of the database's return; nothing has to be reloaded. The home page and a cluster's pages work under it. A page that needs the database shows the sentence where its list would be. **AIOps › Inbox** says *The Inbox cannot be read while the database is unavailable: what waits for you is not known*, above the rows it last read if it had read any, and never *Nothing is pending for you*; the Inbox tab, the bell and the Inbox's Pending filter show a **?** where the count would be, since no count and a plain bell both mean that nothing waits. When the database is back the Inbox reads its lists again at once. In the API every such refusal is a 503 with the header `X-221b-Database: unavailable` and the sentence `the database is unavailable; sign-in and the pages read live from the clusters still work` after what was being read; every GET route answers either 200 or that, the issue service's among them. A person whose only way into AIOps is the issue service's operator role (no console-wide role) sees no AIOps tabs during the outage, because who they are there cannot be read; the banner says why.
- **The move from ConfigMaps** happens once per record set, at the first start with a database. The ConfigMaps are the copy's source and nothing else (DB-69): the console reads them for the copy and writes nothing there. The records are copied in one transaction that also writes the copy down (the `imports` table), so records deleted later do not come back. **Once every record set a ConfigMap held is recorded, the console deletes the ConfigMap** (DB-55, DB-72): each copy names the ConfigMaps it reads and the name it is recorded under, a key read where it is routed and where it was kept before naming both (the audit log in `<release>-audit` and in the settings'); when the wiring is done and every copy has run (the audit log's runs beside the start, and is waited for), each ConfigMap whose record sets are all in `imports` is deleted whole, with any key no record set reads (what the clean start counts, or an older build left). A case's own ConfigMap is deleted whole once the cases' copy is recorded, as before. A copy that fails leaves its ConfigMap whole, every key in it, and the next start copies what is missing and deletes it; a deletion that fails is tried at the next start. A start that finds a ConfigMap of before gone, or never made, takes it for a source with nothing to copy (an installation that started at 0.13 has none); one that finds it made again, empty, records nothing new and deletes it again. From then on the database is the only copy: take a backup in Administration › Backup, and save the ConfigMaps to a file before the first start with a database ([upgrading.md](upgrading.md)).
- **Helm and the ConfigMaps of before** (ADR-167). The chart made them, so a chart that stopped rendering them would have Helm delete them at the upgrade, before the console's copy. This release renders each one only while `lookup` finds it in the namespace, empty, with `helm.sh/resource-policy: keep`; Helm's deletion of an object a chart no longer renders reads the live object and skips one that carries `keep`, and skips one that is gone; an object the chart renders and the cluster lacks, Helm makes. So: the upgrade from 0.12 adds `keep` and leaves the data; the console copies and deletes; the next upgrade renders nothing for it, and Helm neither deletes nor makes it. Where `lookup` cannot see the cluster (`helm template`, a dry run on the client, Argo CD) every one is rendered, with `keep`, so that nothing deletes one before the copy; what such a tool makes again after the deletion is empty, and deleted again at the next start. The console's Role grants `get`, `list` and `delete` on the namespace's ConfigMaps for the copy and the deletion, and `get` and `patch` on the temporary wiki store's ConfigMap by name; it creates, patches and updates no other ConfigMap. The configuration ConfigMaps (`<release>-agents`, `-auth-config`, `-toolbox`) and the wiki's are not record sets and stay.
- **The move from Secrets** follows the same rule with one difference (DB-61): a record set kept in a Secret has a secret part, which stays in the Secret under the names it has, and the rest, which is copied once and then removed from the Secret, alone. No Secret is deleted, but for the session Secrets left with no data (DB-66), and no secret is touched. The credentials' list is the first: the key `index` of `<release>-credentials` becomes rows of `credentials` and is removed, and the `secret-<id>` keys stay (a credential whose name the database has already comes over as *NAME (copied at upgrade)*; a secret no credential names is removed with `DELETE /api/v1/credential-secrets/{id}`, DB-75). The secrets are then of no use without the database, which holds each one's endpoint and kind: save the Secret to a private file before the first start with a database, as well as the ConfigMaps. The database's own backups hold the credentials' names, endpoints and user names from then on; they hold no secret. The applications are the second (DB-62, DB-63): the record under `spec.json` of each `<release>-app-<id>` becomes a row of `applications` and rows of `application_commands`, without its values file, its parameters and its commands' parameters, which are an application's secret part; each Secret is then rewritten in one update, which writes those under `set.json` and removes `spec.json`, and keeps the token and the labels. A Secret whose record could not be read is left as it is. The values are then of no use without the database, which holds what each application is deployed from: the same file of saved Secrets covers them. The database's backups hold the applications' sources, commits and who changed them; they hold no values. The cluster connections are the third (DB-64): the record under `spec.json` of each `<release>-cluster-<id>` becomes a row of `clusters`, and `spec.json` is then removed from the Secret in one update, which touches no key of the credential: the token, the kubeconfig, the client certificate and key, the OIDC secrets and the CA certificate stay where they were, under the names they had. The local cluster's Secret, which held only its saved name, is left with no keys. A Secret whose record could not be read is left as it is. A copy of the connections that fails is tried again every 30 seconds while the console runs, since without it the console shows the local cluster alone. A credential is then of no use without the database, which holds the address it signs in to (a kubeconfig carries its own): the same file of saved Secrets covers it. The database's backups hold the connections' names, addresses and the names their credentials sign in as; they hold no credential. **The sign-in sessions** are the fourth (DB-66), and have no secret part: each session Secret's record is copied into `auth_sessions` if it has not expired, so whoever is signed in stays signed in, and the Secret is then emptied and, holding no data, deleted: the one Secret the move deletes (DB-61, DB-66). An emptied or deleted Secret is no session to any console; one left whole would let a console from before the move take up a session that was signed out since, which is why they are emptied and not kept as a way back. From then on the console makes no session Secret. The database and its backups hold who is signed in from then on (names, emails, groups): personal data, and nothing that signs anyone in, since a row is kept under a hash of the cookie's value. **Automation's sensitive values** are the fifth (DB-71), and go the other way: from the tables into Secrets. Step `0038` adds a digest column beside each value column that may hold one, and moves nothing; the start after it moves every value of a name marked sensitive, on its cluster, out of `variables`, `variable_change_items` and `script_run_values` into the cluster's Secret `<release>-automation-<hash>` (created then), and puts the value's keyed digest in its place; a value quoted in a run's output or in an Automation audit event is blanked. One cluster is one transaction: the rows are changed, the Secret is written, then the transaction commits, so a value is never in neither place, and a cluster whose Secret cannot be written keeps its values, named in the log by cluster, fleet or node and variable, never by value, and is tried again at the next start (every start looks; the first that finishes is recorded as the import `automation/sensitive-values`). From then on the sensitive values are of no use without the database, which holds where each is set, and the database's backups hold no sensitive value: save the Secrets with the ConfigMaps.
- **After a rollback and a roll forward.** A console rolled back to a release without a database finds its ConfigMaps empty of what was copied: it starts with no cases, changes, runs or settings, and nothing that was approved since can be run again from an old copy. It finds the session Secrets empty too, so everyone signs in again, with their accounts as they were. Brought forward again, the sessions it made are not copied (the copy is made once): people sign in once more. The rows of the sessions from before the rollback are still in the table, and would be sessions again, though a sign-out or a password reset made on the rolled-back console did not reach them: **the console ends them itself** at the roll forward's first start (DB-66), when a session Secret holds a session the rolled-back console started more than 10 minutes after the copy, and says so in the log and the audit log (`session.end-all`); everyone signs in again once. A rolled-back console nobody signed in to leaves no such sign, and its sessions are not ended; nothing then needed ending but what an operator did there by hand (a password changed in the accounts' Secret: a local account disabled or deleted is refused all the same, since every request reads the account), and `DELETE FROM auth_sessions` before the roll forward still ends them. Going back therefore means a restore: of the ConfigMaps saved before the upgrade, or of a backup. `helm rollback` makes the ConfigMaps of before again, empty, since the earlier revision renders them, and what a rolled-back console records goes there. Brought forward again to the same database, what only grows is copied: the audit events and the Toolbox's session records newer than the last copy (`imports.newest_at`). Everything else was copied once and is not copied again: for the settings, approvals, inventory and the other records edited in place, the database's are the ones that count, and what the rolled-back console wrote under their keys meanwhile is not read. The ConfigMaps are then deleted again, with it.

## Changing mode

- **No database to bundled or external** (a 0.12 release, or one of mode `none` from a 0.13 build before DB-69): upgrade. The default values give it the bundled database; `database.mode=external` with its host gives it yours. Every record set is copied at the first start ([upgrading.md](upgrading.md)). There is no way back to no database but a rollback of the release, which finds its ConfigMaps made again, empty.
- **One member to three** (`database.bundled.ha: true`): an upgrade; the standbys copy from the primary while it serves. Three to one is allowed and pauses writes briefly.
- **Bundled to external, or back**: take a backup in Administration › Backup, install with the new mode (the chart asks for `database.allowModeChange=true` on a release that had a database), and restore the backup there. Plan it with a maintenance window.
- **What the chart goes by.** The Deployment carries its mode in the annotation `221b.io/database-mode`. A `helm rollback` to a revision from before the database removes it, so the chart also looks for the bundled database's Secret `<release>-db`, which a rollback and an uninstall keep with the volumes: while it is there, `external` is refused, because the records in that database would be left behind. A release whose Deployment has no annotation and that has no such Secret had no database, and gets one with either mode. When the bundled database is no longer wanted, delete the Secrets `<release>-db` and `<release>-db-tls`, the claims `data-<release>-db-*` and `backup-<release>-db-*`, and Patroni's ConfigMaps `<release>-db-config`, `-leader` and `-sync` (and `-failsafe` where there is one); or set `allowModeChange=true`. An external database leaves nothing in the cluster to go by: after a rollback to a revision from before it, the chart cannot tell that there was one.
- **What the database's pods may do in Kubernetes.** Patroni keeps its leader lock in ConfigMaps and labels its pods, so the pods' service account may create, list and watch ConfigMaps and list pods in the namespace, and change only Patroni's own five ConfigMaps (`<release>-db-config`, `-leader`, `-failover`, `-sync`, `-failsafe`) and the database's own pods. Kubernetes cannot limit create, list and watch to names, so the pods can read the namespace's other ConfigMaps; they cannot change them. Patroni runs with one member too: it is the same path as with three, and turning `ha` on is a values change.
- **Stopping a pod of the bundled database** does not wait out its grace period: Patroni hands over or shuts PostgreSQL down, and the backup container ends when it is told to.

## Upgrades

| What | How |
| --- | --- |
| The console | A rolling update; the schema's steps run at start. |
| PostgreSQL's minor version | A new `221b-postgres` image with the release. With three members the pods restart one at a time, and Patroni hands the primary's role over before its pod stops; with one, a restart of under a minute. |
| PostgreSQL's major version | A 221B release line names one major version. Moving to the next is a release's own step, with `pg_upgrade`, described in that release's notes; it is never automatic. |
| An external database | Yours; the console checks the minimum version at start. |

## Writing a domain's repository

Three shapes, by what the service is like:

- **A singleton document** (a page's settings, read whole): a scope of `settings` through `db.ScopeKV`, which is the `kv.Backend` the service already uses. Nothing else to write.
- **A service with few, clear operations**: a repository, as below. This is the end state for every service. The proposed changes have one (`cards.Changes`, DB-79): `Get`, `All`, `Edit` (one change, under its row's lock, writing only what changed of it), `Add` (a proposal, over the changes that wait) and `Change` (the rare edit of every change); `cards.CardsPG` is also the `db.Doc` the one-time copy saves, which takes the turn of every write of the repository (`db.ShareDoc`). The cases are listed by a query (`issues.CasesPG`, DB-88): the issue service's other operations still start from its index, but a list reads only the rows it shows, and a case's save writes only the rows of that case that changed. A job's scheduled time is claimed with `db.Claim` (`jobs.JobsPG.ClaimSlot`, DB-80), as the coordination of the replicas has it (ADR-163).
- **A service that edits one JSON document in many places**: `db.Doc`, an interim step. The service stays as it is; a `Read` builds its document from the model's tables and a `Write` changes the rows from what they hold to what the service saved, every field a column (`twoperson/pg.go`, `cards/pg_approvals.go`). Its records are then rows, keyed and joinable, but the service still reads them all to change one, and writers of one document take turns. Each `db.Doc` is listed in the handover for a repository of its own. A `Write` keeps a record's row, and so its key, for as long as the record is kept: it updates the row in place and writes again only what changed under it, never the record deleted and inserted under a new key (a change, DB-56; a Mycroft run, DB-78; an agent's instructions, DB-81; a known issue and a service, DB-89 and DB-90).

Every domain keeps its records through an interface of its own, defined in its package, with what the services need and nothing of where the records are (ADR-162). `backup.Repo` is the pattern:

1. **Define the interface** from what the service does, not from the tables: `Claim(due, missed)` and `AddRun(run)`, not "load the status, change it, save it".
2. **Keep the storage of before as one implementation** (`KVRepo`, on `kv.Backend`): the one-time copy reads the old records through it, and the tests keep it in memory. Since DB-69 no console keeps records in it.
3. **Write the PostgreSQL implementation** on `internal/db`: `d.Do` for one statement, `d.Tx` for several, `db.LoadScope` and `db.SaveScope` for a singleton, `db.NewID` for keys. Only `internal/db` and the repositories contain SQL.
4. **Test both against one set of tests** (`repo_test.go`); `dbtest.Open(t)` gives each test an empty, migrated database on the server `TEST_DATABASE` names (`make test-db` starts one), and skips without it.
5. **Copy the old records once** with `d.ImportOnce`, and add the domain to the table at the top of this page. A record set that was kept in Secrets keeps its secret part there: its copy and the removal of the part that moved go through `w.moveFromSecrets` (`cmd/221b/database.go`), its test puts a marker in every secret part and asks `dbtest.Holding` that no table holds it (`TestNoSecretIsEverInTheDatabase`), and a row another table points at is updated in place, never deleted and made again (`credentials/pg.go`).
6. **Settle the domain's columns in a new step.** A step is never edited once any database has applied it, a test cluster's included: the database refuses a build whose file differs, and the console does not start. `server/internal/db/testdata/steps.sum` holds every step's checksum and `TestAppliedStepsAreNotEdited` fails on an edit; add the new step's line there. Change [the data model's page](data-model.md) in the same commit: it lists every table and column of the schema with its keys, and `TestTheDataModelIsTheSchema` compares it with a migrated database and fails on any table, column or key that one has and the other does not (DB-68). A step may reshape an earlier step's tables while they hold no rows. A database made by a development build before November 19, 2026 (steps `0001`, `0003`, `0004`, `0006` and `0011` were edited until that day) is made again, empty.
