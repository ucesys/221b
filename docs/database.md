# The database

221B is moving its records from ConfigMaps to PostgreSQL (Phase 13). ConfigMaps hold 1 MiB each, are read and written whole, and gave every record set its own cap; PostgreSQL holds the records as rows of the [data model](data-model.md)'s tables. This page says how the database is chosen and run, what the console does with it, and how a domain's records move.

**Where the move stands.** The database is optional until every domain has moved: `database.mode` is `none` by default, and a console without a database works as before. With one, the domains below keep their records in it and the rest stay in their ConfigMaps.

| Domain | In the database | Still in ConfigMaps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Activity log | the audit log (`audit_events`) and the Toolbox's session records (`toolbox_sessions`): the whole domain | |
| Platform | the shared settings (`settings`, scopes `general`, `sherlock`, `watson`) and the acknowledged alerts (`alert_acks`); the repositories (`repositories`); the backups' settings (scope `backup`) and runs (`backup_runs`); getting started's dismissed steps, Settings › Email and the case work's settings (scopes `setup`, `email`, `cases`); the credentials' list (`credentials`, and the scope `credentials-migration`), which was a key of the credentials' Secret: every field of a credential but its secret, when the secrets are kept in a Secret; the applications (`applications`, `application_commands`), which were a key of each application's Secret: every field of an application but its values file, its parameters and its commands' parameters, when the connections are kept in Secrets; the cluster connections (`clusters`), which were a key of each connection's Secret: every field of a connection but its credential, when the connections are kept in Secrets | what stays in Secrets for good: every credential's secret, in the credentials' Secret; every application's values, parameters and commands' parameters, in the application's Secret; and every connection's credential (the token, kubeconfig, client certificate and key, OIDC secrets, with the CA certificate and the rest that is read with them), in the connection's Secret. `clusters` holds a row with only its id for each cluster another table points at whose connection it does not keep, and `credentials` one for each credential something names that the list does not have |

`GET /api/v1/database` and Administration's metrics say the same of a running console.

## Choosing a database

`database.mode` in the chart's values:

| Mode | What runs | For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `none` | Nothing. | The default until the move is complete. |
| `bundled` | The chart's own PostgreSQL beside the console, from the image `221b-postgres`: one member, or three with `database.bundled.ha`. | An installation with no PostgreSQL to use. |
| `external` | Nothing of the chart's; the console connects to a PostgreSQL you run. | A managed service (RDS, Cloud SQL, Azure), your own Patroni or CloudNativePG cluster, a central database platform. |

The chart refuses what cannot work: a mode that is none of the three; `external` with no host, or with `sslMode: disable` and no `allowInsecure`; one bundled member in a namespace labelled `221b.io/environment=production` without `database.bundled.acknowledgeNoHA`; and a release whose mode changes between `bundled` and `external`, which is a migration (below), not a value.

## The bundled database

One release, two images: `221b` stays distroless and holds no database; `221b-postgres` holds PostgreSQL 18 from the PostgreSQL project's packages, [Patroni](https://patroni.readthedocs.io/) and [WAL-G](https://github.com/wal-g/wal-g) on Debian, and runs as user 999 with a read-only root filesystem.

- **Members.** A StatefulSet of one pod, or three with `ha: true`, each with its own volume (`storage.size`, 10 GiB by default). Three members are kept on different nodes, spread over zones where there are zones, with a disruption budget of one. Turning `ha` on later adds the two standbys while the primary keeps serving.
- **Who leads.** Patroni keeps its leader lock and the cluster's state in ConfigMaps of the console's namespace and labels each pod with its role; the Service `<release>-db` selects the pod labelled `primary`, and is the console's one address. A dead primary is replaced within about 30 seconds; a planned switch takes a few. While the Kubernetes API is away the primary keeps serving as long as it reaches every member (failsafe mode).
- **Commits.** With `synchronous: quorum` (the default) a commit waits for one of the two standbys, so a failover loses nothing committed, and losing one standby stops nothing. One member alone waits for nobody.
- **Access.** The chart makes three passwords once, in the Secret `<release>-db`, and keeps them through upgrades and an uninstall, as the volumes are kept: the superuser's and the replication role's are Patroni's, and the console is given only its own role's, `app_221b`, which owns the database `221b` and nothing else. Connections are TLS with a certificate the chart makes once (`<release>-db-tls`; or yours, `tls.secretName`), verified by the console (`verify-full`), and passwords are SCRAM. A network policy admits the console and the members to PostgreSQL, and the members alone to Patroni's API.
- **Kubernetes rights.** A Role in the namespace: ConfigMaps, and its own pods to label. Nothing cluster-wide, no CRDs, no operator.
- **Backups.** PostgreSQL archives every completed segment of its write-ahead log through WAL-G, and a `backup` container in each pod takes a base backup once a day (from `backup.dailyAt`, UTC) on one member. `backup.retainBaseBackups` (7) are kept, with the log between them, so the database can be restored to any moment since the oldest. Both are encrypted before they leave the pod, with the key `backup-key` of the Secret `<release>-db`.
  - **To a bucket** (`backup.s3`): every member writes to the one archive. The standby whose name sorts first takes the base backup, or the primary when it is alone.
  - **To a volume** (no bucket): each member has a backup volume of its own, and the log goes to the primary's. So the primary takes the base backup, and a member that becomes the primary after another one was takes one at once: its volume has no log for the time in between. The first primary takes one as soon as it runs. After a failover the time before it can be restored only from the old primary's volume; with three members, a bucket is the better place. A volume does not survive losing the cluster's storage, and the chart's notes say so.
- **Restore.** A new installation starts from the archive with `database.bundled.restore.fromBackup=true`, to the newest point or to `restore.targetTime` (RFC 3339, in quotes, with a time zone: `"2026-11-20T02:19:29Z"` or with an offset; the chart refuses a time without one and hands PostgreSQL the offset in numbers, since PostgreSQL refuses `Z` there); remove the value after the first install. **For a restore in place**, after `helm uninstall` delete Patroni's ConfigMaps too (`<release>-db-config`, `-leader`, `-failover`, `-sync`, `-failsafe`): they are Patroni's, not the chart's, and outlive the uninstall, and a new installation that finds them takes itself for the old cluster. A restore needs what the backups were made with:
  - **The key.** The archive is read with the `backup-key` of the old installation's Secret `<release>-db`. Under the same release name and namespace the kept Secret is found again and nothing is needed. Under another name, in another namespace or in another cluster, the chart would make a new key, and the restore would fail: copy the old Secret there first and name it in `database.bundled.existingSecret`. Copy it whole, since the restored database knows the old passwords.
  - **The archive.** A bucket is reached from anywhere with the same `backup.s3` values. A backup volume belongs to its release: it is found again only under the same release name and namespace, where the claim `backup-<release>-db-0` is kept. The restore reads the volume of `<release>-db-0`, so that member must be the primary before the uninstall, with a base backup of its own: after an upgrade that restarted the database's pods another member usually leads (`patronictl list`), and `patronictl switchover --leader <it> --candidate <release>-db-0 --force` puts it back; `db-0` takes a base backup at once. To restore there, uninstall, delete the data claim `data-<release>-db-0` (and those of the other members), keep the backup claim and the Secret, and install with `restore.fromBackup=true`.
  - Keep a copy of the Secret outside the cluster: without `backup-key` the backups cannot be read by anyone.
  - **After a restore that failed.** A restore that PostgreSQL cannot start from (a wrong `targetTime`, a key that does not open the archive) leaves the database's pod starting over and over with `Failed to bootstrap cluster` in its log, and `helm install --wait` gives up. Nothing of the archive is changed by it. Correct the value and run `helm upgrade` with the same restore values: the member starts again from the archive, not from what the failed attempt left. Each failed attempt moves the data directory it had begun aside, to `pgdata.failed` beside `pgdata` on the member's data volume (`/var/lib/postgresql/data/pgdata.failed` in the pod), in place of the one before, so there is one of them, the size of the base backup it had fetched (40 MB in the seventh cluster test, beside a 249 MB `pgdata`). Nothing reads it afterwards, and nothing removes it: it stays through the restore that works, restarts of the pod and upgrades, and goes only with the data claim. Once the restored database serves, remove it by hand: `kubectl exec <release>-db-0 -c postgres -- rm -rf /var/lib/postgresql/data/pgdata.failed`. Look at it first only if the restore still fails and the log does not say why: its `postgresql.conf` and `log` are what PostgreSQL was given.

## An external database

The console needs, of a PostgreSQL you run:

- **PostgreSQL 16 or later.** The console checks at start and does not start on an older one.
- **A database and a role that owns it**, made beforehand (`database.external.database`, `user`; `221b` and `app_221b` by default). The console makes and changes its own tables as that role; it needs no superuser, no replication right and nothing outside its database. No extension is needed.
- **One address that reaches the primary** (`host`, `port`): for CloudNativePG the cluster's `-rw` Service, for Patroni the primary's Service or a load balancer that follows it. The console connects again when the primary changes.
- **TLS.** `sslMode` is `verify-full` by default, with the CA in `caSecret` (key `ca.crt`) unless a public CA signed the server's certificate. `clientCertSecret` (`tls.crt`, `tls.key`) is for a server that asks for a client certificate.
- **The password** in a Secret (`passwordSecret.name` and `key`). It is read at every new connection, so a rotated Secret needs no restart.
- **Connections.** `maxConnections` (20) is what all the console's replicas open between them; each replica also holds up to four direct sessions for its listener and its locks.
- **`track_commit_timestamp = on`** is recommended; the console warns when it is off.

**Poolers.** The console listens for notices (`LISTEN`) and takes session advisory locks; PgBouncer in transaction mode keeps neither. Session mode, or no pooler, needs nothing. With a pooler in transaction mode, give `listenerHost`, a direct address: the listener, the locks and the migrations use it, and everything else goes through the pooler. The console's own pool is small; most installations need no pooler.

Availability and backups of an external database are yours. The console's own backup (Administration › Backup) works in every mode and is what moves an installation between modes.

## What the console does with it

- **At start** it connects (trying for two minutes, since a bundled database may still be starting), checks the version, and brings the schema to its own: the steps are embedded in the binary and applied in order behind one advisory lock, so replicas that start together migrate one after another. Within a release line the steps only add, so the replicas of the release before go on working during a rollout, and going back one release is safe. The console does not listen until this is done, and the chart's startup probe allows for it (seven and a half minutes with a database), so the liveness probe does not stop a console that is waiting for its database. **When the database has not answered in the two minutes, the console starts without it** (DB-67, below), unless `database.startWithoutIt` is `false`: then it exits, the pod starts again, and nothing listens for as long as the database stays away.
- **No replica leads the database's work.** A scheduled time is claimed by inserting its row, and the replica whose insert goes in runs it; a pass over everything runs behind an advisory lock of its name; a change the other replicas should see is announced with `NOTIFY` and looked for anyway every `database.pollInterval` (30 seconds), so a lost notice delays a change and never loses it. The Lease that elects the replica reading the clusters stays until the conversations and cards it protects have moved too.
- **While the database is away** the console stays ready and goes on serving what does not need it: sign-in, and every page read live from Kubernetes. **Sign-in** needs only the accounts' Secret, the directory or the provider, and somewhere to keep the session. That place is the database (the table `auth_sessions`, DB-66); without one it is the session Secrets, so the Kubernetes API server. When it cannot be written, the session is kept in its cookie instead, signed with the key in the Secret `<release>-session-key`, and every replica takes it without the store (DB-65). The sign-in page then says so: the session lasts `auth.outageSession`, 30 minutes from sign-in, is not extended by use, and becomes an ordinary one at the first request after the store is back, without a new sign-in. Until then it cannot be ended everywhere at once: a sign-out ends it on the replica that served it, a password reset on the replicas that hold it, and a local account disabled or deleted loses it at once everywhere. A person already signed in goes on, on the replica that has seen them, from that replica's memory; on another replica they are asked to sign in, and get a signed session. Nobody waits for a database that does not answer: a read or write of a session is given 2 seconds, and a database found away is left alone for 10 seconds and then tried by one request at a time, so one that swallows what it is sent costs one request in ten seconds those 2 seconds, and one that refuses costs nothing. A sign-out during the outage is refused at once on the replica that served it, which removes the session's row as soon as the database's health says it is back; the other replicas, which could not be told, take the session until then (a replica restarted meanwhile forgets what it was to remove, and the row then lives until its idle limit). Last activity is not written during the outage; each replica counts idle time from what it has seen. A person in so many groups that the session does not fit a cookie is told that a local account can sign in. A page that needs the database says it is unavailable; a write fails with a sentence and is never queued in memory, except the audit log, whose newest 2,000 events wait. `b221_database_up` is 0, and the log says when it left and when it came back. **The clusters stay connected** (DB-64): every connection that is running keeps running with the credential it has, and the local cluster never needs the database at all; the log says once that the saved connections cannot be read, and once that they can again. Adding, changing or removing a connection is refused with why, and so are a test of a saved connection and a backup of the connections, which read the saved credential. What another replica changed just before is read when the database answers. A console that starts while the database is away does not start (DB-02), so the question of connections that were never loaded does not arise.
- **A console that starts while the database is away** (DB-67, ADR-166) waits its two minutes and then starts without it, so that the console is there when it is wanted most: to see why the database's pods do not start. It is ready when its cluster is listed, like any console. **What works**: sign-in (the session is a signed one, as above, and the sign-in page says so), the local cluster's pages, Administration › Users, health and metrics. **What does not**: every page that needs the database says it is unavailable (the API answers 503 with the sentence, at once); **the cluster menu shows the local cluster alone**, because the other connections are rows of the database; the settings in force are the chart's and not the saved ones, with the cautious choice where a setting allows something (no registry is asked about newer images, costs are hidden); Watson changes nothing and Sherlock is off; no job, backup or pass of the case work runs. `GET /api/v1/database` says `startedWithout`, and `b221_database_started_without` is 1. **Such a console keeps no records.** It does not migrate, copy or remove; its database handle refuses every call without sending anything, also once the database is back; and as itself it writes no ConfigMap and no Secret but the local accounts' Secret and the session key's (the API server is not even asked). It never reads a ConfigMap in the database's place: they were emptied by the move, and a console that fell back to them would start with nothing and write new records where an older console would find them. **When the database answers**, which the console asks every 5 seconds, it logs `the database answers: this console was started without it and stops now`, stops as it does on a `SIGTERM`, and exits with code 0; the kubelet starts the container again (the pod's restart count goes up by one, its last state reads `Completed`), and that start is an ordinary one, with the schema's steps and the copies. People signed in meanwhile stay signed in: their signed session is exchanged for a stored one at their next request. Two things are lost: the audit events of that time are in the audit stream and the webhook, and on the Audit log page only until the restart (it shows them from memory), and a session made before the outage is not known to such a console, so its person signs in again. With two replicas of which one was started without the database and one with it, the one with it works as ever and the other says the database is unavailable; a person who reaches both is asked to sign in more than once, until the second has started again.
- **The move from ConfigMaps** happens once per record set, at the first start with a database: the records are copied in one transaction that also writes the copy down (the `imports` table), so records deleted later do not come back. **Once the copy is recorded, the record set is removed from its ConfigMap**: the key is deleted and the ConfigMap stays, since the chart makes it; a case's own ConfigMap is deleted whole. A record set an earlier build copied and left is removed at the next start. A copy that fails leaves the ConfigMap as it was, and the next start tries again. From then on the database is the only copy: take a backup in Administration › Backup, and save the ConfigMaps to a file before the first start with a database ([upgrading.md](upgrading.md)).
- **The move from Secrets** follows the same rule with one difference (DB-61): a record set kept in a Secret has a secret part, which stays in the Secret under the names it has, and the rest, which is copied once and then removed from the Secret, alone. No Secret is deleted and no secret is touched. The credentials' list is the first: the key `index` of `<release>-credentials` becomes rows of `credentials` and is removed, and the `secret-<id>` keys stay. The secrets are then of no use without the database, which holds each one's endpoint and kind: save the Secret to a private file before the first start with a database, as well as the ConfigMaps. The database's own backups hold the credentials' names, endpoints and user names from then on; they hold no secret. The applications are the second (DB-62, DB-63): the record under `spec.json` of each `<release>-app-<id>` becomes a row of `applications` and rows of `application_commands`, without its values file, its parameters and its commands' parameters, which are an application's secret part; each Secret is then rewritten in one update, which writes those under `set.json` and removes `spec.json`, and keeps the token and the labels. A Secret whose record could not be read is left as it is. The values are then of no use without the database, which holds what each application is deployed from: the same file of saved Secrets covers them. The database's backups hold the applications' sources, commits and who changed them; they hold no values. The cluster connections are the third (DB-64): the record under `spec.json` of each `<release>-cluster-<id>` becomes a row of `clusters`, and `spec.json` is then removed from the Secret in one update, which touches no key of the credential: the token, the kubeconfig, the client certificate and key, the OIDC secrets and the CA certificate stay where they were, under the names they had. The local cluster's Secret, which held only its saved name, is left with no keys. A Secret whose record could not be read is left as it is. A copy of the connections that fails is tried again every 30 seconds while the console runs, since without it the console shows the local cluster alone. A credential is then of no use without the database, which holds the address it signs in to (a kubeconfig carries its own): the same file of saved Secrets covers it. The database's backups hold the connections' names, addresses and the names their credentials sign in as; they hold no credential. **The sign-in sessions** are the fourth (DB-66), and have no secret part: each session Secret's record is copied into `auth_sessions` if it has not expired, so whoever is signed in stays signed in, and the Secret is then emptied and left, an object with its labels and no keys. An emptied Secret is no session to any console; one left whole would let a console from before the move take up a session that was signed out since, which is why they are emptied and not kept as a way back. From then on the console makes no session Secret. The database and its backups hold who is signed in from then on (names, emails, groups): personal data, and nothing that signs anyone in, since a row is kept under a hash of the cookie's value.
- **After a rollback and a roll forward.** A console rolled back to a release without a database finds its ConfigMaps empty of what was copied: it starts with no cases, changes, runs or settings, and nothing that was approved since can be run again from an old copy. It finds the session Secrets empty too, so everyone signs in again, with their accounts as they were. Brought forward again, the sessions it made are not copied (the copy is made once): people sign in once more. The rows of the sessions from before the rollback are still in the table, and one that has not been idle for longer than `auth.sessionIdle` (8 hours) is a session again, for whoever still holds its cookie: a sign-out or a password reset made on the rolled-back console did not reach it (a local account disabled or deleted there is refused all the same, since every request reads the account). After a rollback during which sessions had to be ended, empty the table before the roll forward's first start: `DELETE FROM auth_sessions`, which signs out nobody who is not already asked to sign in. Going back therefore means a restore: of the ConfigMaps saved before the upgrade, or of a backup. What a rolled-back console records goes to its ConfigMaps. Brought forward again to the same database, what only grows is copied: the audit events and the Toolbox's session records newer than the last copy (`imports.newest_at`), which then leave the ConfigMap again. Everything else was copied once and is not copied again: for the settings, approvals, inventory and the other records edited in place, the database's are the ones that count, and what the rolled-back console wrote under their keys meanwhile is removed at the next start with the database, not read.

## Changing mode

- **None to bundled or external**: set the mode. Each domain that has moved copies its records at the next start.
- **One member to three** (`database.bundled.ha: true`): an upgrade; the standbys copy from the primary while it serves. Three to one is allowed and pauses writes briefly.
- **Bundled to external, or back**: take a backup in Administration › Backup, install with the new mode (the chart asks for `database.allowModeChange=true` on a release that had a database), and restore the backup there. Plan it with a maintenance window.
- **What the chart goes by.** The Deployment carries its mode in the annotation `221b.io/database-mode`. A `helm rollback` to a revision from before the database removes it, so the chart also looks for the bundled database's Secret `<release>-db`, which a rollback and an uninstall keep with the volumes: while it is there, any mode but `bundled` is refused, because the records in that database would be left behind. When the bundled database is no longer wanted, delete the Secrets `<release>-db` and `<release>-db-tls`, the claims `data-<release>-db-*` and `backup-<release>-db-*`, and Patroni's ConfigMaps `<release>-db-config`, `-leader` and `-sync` (and `-failsafe` where there is one); or set `allowModeChange=true`. An external database leaves nothing in the cluster to go by: after a rollback to a revision from before it, the chart cannot tell that there was one.
- **What the database's pods may do in Kubernetes.** Patroni keeps its leader lock in ConfigMaps and labels its pods, so the pods' service account may create, list and watch ConfigMaps and list pods in the namespace, and change only Patroni's own five ConfigMaps (`<release>-db-config`, `-leader`, `-failover`, `-sync`, `-failsafe`) and the database's own pods. Kubernetes cannot limit create, list and watch to names, so the pods can read the namespace's other ConfigMaps; they cannot change them. Patroni runs with one member too: it is the same path as with three, and turning `ha` on is a values change.
- **Stopping a pod of the bundled database** does not wait out its grace period: Patroni hands over or shuts PostgreSQL down, and the backup container ends when it is told to.

## Upgrades

| What | How |
| --- | --- |
| The console | A rolling update; the schema's steps run at start. |
| PostgreSQL's minor version | A new `221b-postgres` image with the release. With three members the pods restart one at a time, and Patroni hands the primary's role over before its pod stops; with one, a restart of under a minute. |
| PostgreSQL's major version | A 221B release line names one major version. Moving to the next is a release's own step, with `pg_upgrade`, described in that release's notes; it is never automatic. |
| An external database | Yours; the console checks the minimum version at start. |

## Writing a domain's repository

Three shapes, by what the service is like:

- **A singleton document** (a page's settings, read whole): a scope of `settings` through `db.ScopeKV`, which is the `kv.Backend` the service already uses. Nothing else to write.
- **A service with few, clear operations**: a repository, as below. This is the end state for every service.
- **A service that edits one JSON document in many places**: `db.Doc`, an interim step. The service stays as it is; a `Read` builds its document from the model's tables and a `Write` changes the rows from what they hold to what the service saved, every field a column (`twoperson/pg.go`, `cards/pg_approvals.go`). Its records are then rows, keyed and joinable, but the service still reads them all to change one, and writers of one document take turns. Each `db.Doc` is listed in the handover for a repository of its own.

Every domain keeps its records through an interface of its own, defined in its package, with what the services need and nothing of where the records are (ADR-162). `backup.Repo` is the pattern:

1. **Define the interface** from what the service does, not from the tables: `Claim(due, missed)` and `AddRun(run)`, not "load the status, change it, save it".
2. **Keep today's storage as one implementation** (`KVRepo`, on `kv.Backend`), so a console without a database, the simulator and the tests work as before.
3. **Write the PostgreSQL implementation** on `internal/db`: `d.Do` for one statement, `d.Tx` for several, `db.LoadScope` and `db.SaveScope` for a singleton, `db.NewID` for keys. Only `internal/db` and the repositories contain SQL.
4. **Test both against one set of tests** (`repo_test.go`); `dbtest.Open(t)` gives each test an empty, migrated database on the server `TEST_DATABASE` names (`make test-db` starts one), and skips without it.
5. **Copy the old records once** with `d.ImportOnce`, and add the domain to the table at the top of this page. A record set that was kept in Secrets keeps its secret part there: its copy and the removal of the part that moved go through `w.moveFromSecrets` (`cmd/221b/database.go`), its test puts a marker in every secret part and asks `dbtest.Holding` that no table holds it (`TestNoSecretIsEverInTheDatabase`), and a row another table points at is updated in place, never deleted and made again (`credentials/pg.go`).
6. **Settle the domain's columns in a new step.** A step is never edited once any database has applied it, a test cluster's included: the database refuses a build whose file differs, and the console does not start. `server/internal/db/testdata/steps.sum` holds every step's checksum and `TestAppliedStepsAreNotEdited` fails on an edit; add the new step's line there. A step may reshape an earlier step's tables while they hold no rows. A database made by a development build before November 19, 2026 (steps `0001`, `0003`, `0004`, `0006` and `0011` were edited until that day) is made again, empty.
